# Appendix C: Getting Your Season Map from Free Tools

You need three things from any tool: the Moon's star at your birth, the list of your nine seasons with dates, and the sub-season table for the seasons you want to read. Two free tools give all three. Menus move between versions, so where I say "look for" rather than "click", that is why.

## What to set, in any tool

Before you read a single date, check three settings. They are usually already right in Indian software and sometimes wrong in Western apps.

1. **Zodiac: sidereal.** The star-fixed zodiac that Vedic astrology uses. If the tool offers "tropical", that is the Western one; do not use it.
2. **The standard Indian setting: Lahiri** (called the ayanamsa in the menus; also written Chitrapaksha or Chitra Paksha). Every date in this book assumes it. A different setting moves the Moon by up to a degree or two and can shift your balance at birth by months.
3. **The timetable: the 120-year one (Vimshottari).** Tools offer several timetables; ignore the others for now.

Also use whole-sign rooms (one sign, one room), which is the Vedic default, so that your rooms match Chapter 13.

## Jagannatha Hora (Windows, free)

This is the program most serious Indian astrologers use, and it costs nothing. Download it from its author's site (search "Jagannatha Hora download"; the program is by P. V. R. Narasimha Rao). It runs on Windows; on a Mac it needs a Windows layer.

1. Open the program. The first dialog, or the **Birth data** item under the first menu, asks for name, date, time and place. Type the city and pick it from the list so the longitude, latitude and time zone fill in; check that the time zone matches the place and year (India is +5:30).
2. Open **Preferences**, then the section on calculations. Set the zodiac to sidereal and the setting to Lahiri. Set the house system to whole sign. Save, and tick the option that keeps these as defaults so you do not do this again.
3. The main window shows the chart. Look for the Moon's star: the panel of planet positions lists each planet with its sign, degree and star, and the star's lord. Write the Moon's star and lord down; that is your first season.
4. Click the **Dasas** tab along the top of the window. In the list of dasha systems on the left, choose the 120-year timetable (spelt "Vimsottari" there). The right-hand panel shows your nine seasons with start and end dates.
5. Click a season to open its nine sub-seasons; click a sub-season to open the small turns inside it. The dates appear in the same panel.
6. To keep it: right-click in the panel for a copy option and paste into a document, or use the print and save options under the first menu (the program can save the whole chart as a web page). A screenshot of the open panel works just as well.

## The AstroSage app (Android and iPhone, free)

1. Install **AstroSage Kundli** and open it. Choose **Kundli**, then the option to create a new one. Enter name, date, time and place; pick the place from the suggestions so the coordinates fill in.
2. Open the app's settings (the gear icon) and check that the chart style is one you can read (north or south), the zodiac is sidereal and the setting is Lahiri (Chitrapaksha). It usually is.
3. Open the chart you made. The screen has tabs along the top or bottom. The **Basic** tab lists the Moon's sign and star ("Nakshatra") and the star's lord: your first season.
4. Find the **Dasha** tab. The first screen lists your nine seasons (labelled "Mahadasha") with start and end dates. Tap a season to see its sub-seasons (labelled "Antardasha"), and tap a sub-season to see the small turns inside it.
5. To keep it: take a screenshot of each screen you need (the seasons list, and the sub-season list for your current season and the past seasons you want to read). Some versions offer a shareable report as a file; the screenshot is enough.

## Drik Panchang, in one line

The Drik Panchang website also casts a chart with the 120-year season table under its "Kundali" tools, and its day-by-day panchang pages are the quickest way to check which star the Moon was in on your date of birth.

## If the birth time is uncertain

Most people know their birth time to within an hour or two; some know only "morning" or "night"; a few know only the day. Here is what that does to your map, so you know what you can trust.

**The first season rarely changes; the balance does.** The Moon moves about 13° a day, a sign every 2¼ days, and a star (13°20′) in about a day. So if your time is wrong by an hour, the Moon is wrong by about half a degree, which is a small part of a star: your first season is almost always the same, but the balance at birth shifts. The rule of thumb: an hour's error moves every later date on your map by about 4 per cent of your first season's full length. That is about ten months if your first season is Venus (20 years), nine if Saturn, eight or nine if Rahu or Mercury, eight if Jupiter, five if the Moon, three if the Sun, Mars or Ketu. A whole morning's uncertainty can be a year or more. Changeover dates are the ones to hold loosely.

**If the Moon sits near the edge of a star,** within a degree or two, a modest error can push it into the next star and change the first season entirely. The apps show the degree; if your Moon's degree is within 1° of a star boundary in Table A.3, treat your first season as uncertain and read both possibilities against your life.

**Fit the clock to the life.** Cast the chart for the earliest and latest times you think possible. Compare the sub-season dates each gives for two or three events you can date exactly (a marriage, a first job, a child). The time whose sub-seasons fit the events best is the one to use. This is a plain version of what astrologers call rectification, and it is honest work, not cheating, as long as you fit to events you already know and not to hopes.

**If you know only the day,** cast the chart for noon. The Moon will be within about 6½° of its true place, which keeps it inside one star more often than not, and the first season will usually be right. The rising sign, the rooms and the exact dates will not be; read the seasons as themes and keep the dates to the nearest year or two.

**If the day itself is uncertain,** as it is for some older relatives, the timetable cannot be built. Read the weather (Chapter 14) and the planets' natures instead, and be honest with the person about why.

Whatever you find, write the time you used and the settings you chose on the map itself, so that when you redraw it in a year you draw the same map.
