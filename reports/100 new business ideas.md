# 100 New Business Ideas for India

**Every idea here already works and makes money in another country, and is rare or missing in India.** None of them repeats the earlier list. Each one is explained on its own card in plain words.

## How to read each card

- **In one line:** what the business is.
- **What's missing in India today:** the everyday problem it solves.
- **How it works:** what the customer does, what you do, and who does the actual work. In every idea, a partner, freelancer or supplier does the hands-on work while you find customers and run things.
- **Who pays and how much:** the customer and a rough price.
- **Proof it works abroad:** a real company doing it, with a link.
- **Who does this in India now:** the Indian businesses we found doing the same thing.
- **To start:** roughly how much money you need, how many weeks until the first paying customer, and the biggest risk.

## Three honest notes

1. **The competitor checks are quick web searches.** Small businesses that only sell on Instagram don't show up in them. Before you choose an idea, spend half a day searching Instagram, Justdial and Google Maps in your own city.
2. **Every price and cost marked "about" is an estimate**, not a quote from a supplier.
3. A few "proof abroad" lines are marked "(not checked)" because we could not open a source for them.

## Health, wellness and beauty

### 1. Head-lice removal at home for children
**In one line:** Trained women visit homes and comb out head lice and their eggs, without harsh chemicals.

**What's missing in India today:** Lice are common in school children, especially girls with long hair, and they spread to the whole family. Parents spend hours combing or buy strong lotions, because no one offers a discreet service that does the job for them.

**How it works:**
1. A parent books a visit on WhatsApp, often after a note from school.
2. You recruit women (for example, salon helpers or nannies) and train them to comb hair section by section with fine metal lice combs and conditioner.
3. Your partner arrives with a plain-looking kit, checks every family member, and combs out the lice and eggs in about 1–2 hours.
4. You sell a follow-up check 7–10 days later, and offer "lice check days" to schools and girls' hostels.

**Who pays and how much:** Parents in big cities; about ₹1,500–2,500 per child per visit, plus about ₹800 for the follow-up check.

**Proof it works abroad:** Lice Clinics of America, USA: had done 750,000 head-lice treatments and had 179 clinics in 17 countries by 2021 ([source](https://www.globenewswire.com/fr/news-release/2021/10/27/2321835/0/en/Lice-Clinics-of-America-hits-750K-treatments.html))

**Who does this in India now:** No one found in a quick search.

**To start:** About ₹50,000 (combs, training, kits, Reels ads). About 3–4 weeks to the first paying family. Main risk: parents feel embarrassed to book, so everything must look discreet. (The US chain uses an approved heated-air machine; you would use combing only and never promise a "cure".)

### 2. Stretch coaches who come to your home
**In one line:** A trained coach visits and gently moves your body through deep stretches while you lie on a mat.

**What's missing in India today:** Desk workers, older adults and weekend players feel stiff, but their only options are a massage, a physiotherapist (for injuries) or a yoga class where they stretch themselves. No one sells one-on-one sessions where someone else stretches you.

**How it works:**
1. The customer books a 45-minute session or a pack of 8.
2. You hire certified fitness trainers or yoga teachers and pay for a short course in assisted stretching.
3. Your partner brings a mat and straps, fills in a health form with the customer, and stretches their legs, hips, back and shoulders.
4. You handle bookings, reminders and packs, and send anyone with pain or an injury to a doctor instead.

**Who pays and how much:** Office workers aged 30–60, golfers, runners, and older parents (often paid for by their children); about ₹1,200–1,800 per session, or about ₹9,000 for 8.

**Proof it works abroad:** StretchLab, USA: became the first assisted-stretching chain to reach 500 studios in North America, in 2024 ([source](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20241022781478/en/StretchLab-Becomes-First-Assisted-Stretching-Franchise-to-Reach-500-Studios-In-North-America))

**Who does this in India now:** No dedicated stretching service found. Nearest: Tattva Spa sells a "yoga stretch therapy" spa treatment ([link](https://tattvaspa.com/product/signature-yoga-stretch-therapy/)), and physiotherapy clinics stretch patients as part of treatment.

**To start:** About ₹60,000 (course for 2–3 coaches, mats, straps, ads). About 3–4 weeks. Main risk: a customer gets hurt, so coaches need proper training and a health form before every first session.

### 3. Workout classes for new mothers, with their babies
**In one line:** Group exercise classes where new mothers work out while their babies sit in prams or carriers beside them.

**What's missing in India today:** After a baby, many mothers want to exercise and meet other mothers but cannot leave the baby at home. Gyms don't allow babies, and postnatal classes here are mostly yoga sessions where the baby stays home.

**How it works:**
1. A mother joins a monthly plan once her doctor says she can exercise (usually 6 or more weeks after birth).
2. You find morning slots in housing-society clubhouses, malls before opening time, or shaded parks.
3. Your partner, a certified pregnancy and after-birth fitness trainer, runs a 45–60 minute class of brisk walking, squats and tummy work, with songs for the babies.
4. You run the mothers' WhatsApp group, sell monthly plans, and add coffee meet-ups after class.

**Who pays and how much:** Mothers of babies aged about 2 months to 2 years; about ₹3,000–4,000 a month for 3 classes a week, or about ₹500 for a single class.

**Proof it works abroad:** FIT4MOM (Stroller Strides), USA: had 221 locations open, according to its 2024 franchise papers ([source](https://franzy.com/franchises/fit4mom))

**Who does this in India now:** No pram or baby-carrier workout classes found. Nearest: Birth N Beyond Yoga in Mumbai runs postnatal exercise sessions without babies ([link](https://playsport.com/birthnbeyondyoga_ag_34045f78)).

**To start:** About ₹40,000 (trainer fees for a trial month, venue, ads). About 3–4 weeks. Main risk: heat and pollution, so you need indoor clubhouse slots as a back-up.

### 4. Stylish reusable earplugs for noisy Indian cities
**In one line:** Soft, good-looking reusable earplugs that turn down loud noise but still let you hear voices and music.

**What's missing in India today:** Honking, wedding bands, DJ trucks, building work and festival crackers are very loud, and many people sleep badly because of noise. Most people only know cheap foam plugs from chemists, which look odd and block everything.

**How it works:**
1. You find a factory (in China or India) that makes silicone earplugs and have it make them in your colours and case, under your brand.
2. You sell online with Reels about everyday noise: sleeping, concerts, weddings, metro rides, focusing at work.
3. Customers buy one pair, or a set (one for sleep, one for events).
4. You add sales stands at concerts, gyms and shared offices.

**Who pays and how much:** City people aged 18–45: concert-goers, light sleepers, night-shift workers, parents at weddings; about ₹999–1,599 a pair.

**Proof it works abroad:** Loop Earplugs, Belgium: sales grew to €126.5 million in 2023 and €190 million in 2024 ([source](https://www.bigblue.co/blog/loop-earplugs-from-eu0-to-eu42m-targeting-ads-and-community))

**Who does this in India now:** No Indian brand found. Loop's own earplugs reach India only through import websites such as Ubuy ([link](https://www.ubuy.co.in/product/3LMG0NO-loop-quiet-ear-plugs-for-noise-reduction-super-soft-reusable-hearing-protection-in-flexible-silicone-for-sleep-noise-sensitivity-8-ear-tips-in)).

**To start:** About ₹1.5–2 lakh (first batch of about 1,000 pairs, samples, packaging, ads). About 8–10 weeks. Main risk: Loop could launch properly in India and outspend you.

### 5. Self-warming steam eye masks
**In one line:** Use-once eye masks that gently warm up when you open them, giving 20 minutes of relaxing heat.

**What's missing in India today:** Office workers and students stare at screens all day, and many sleep badly. In Japan these masks are an everyday habit, but in India they are only sold as random foreign imports.

**How it works:**

2. You sell boxes online with Reels: "after work", "before sleep", "on a flight".
3. Customers buy boxes of 5 or 10, or a monthly box.
4. You pitch them as gifts, and to hotels and airport lounges.

**Who pays and how much:** Desk workers, students and frequent flyers; about ₹499–699 for a box of 10.

**Proof it works abroad:** Kao MegRhythm, Japan: Kao says its steam eye mask is No. 1 by sales value in Japan's eye-care market (Intage data, 2020–2022) ([source](https://www.kao.com/th/en/products/megrhythm/mrm_hoteyemask_00/))

**Who does this in India now:** No Indian brand found in a quick search; only foreign packs sold by importers.

**To start:** About ₹1.2–1.5 lakh (first order, safety tests on the heat pads, ads). About 8–10 weeks. Main risk: the masks must never get too hot, and you must not make health claims about eyes.

### 6. Refillable deodorant with refills by post
**In one line:** Buy one smart deodorant case once, then get cheaper refills delivered every two months.

**What's missing in India today:** Every underarm deodorant in Indian shops comes in a plastic bottle or can that goes in the bin when empty. There is almost no option to keep one good case and buy only the refill.

**How it works:**
1. The customer buys a starter kit: a sturdy case plus one refill.
2. A licensed cosmetics factory (a factory that makes products under your brand name) makes the refills from one of its ready recipes; a packaging supplier makes the case.
3. You post refills every 2 months, and customers can pause or skip any time.
4. You add new scents and gift sets to keep people buying.

**Who pays and how much:** City people aged 20–40 who want less plastic; about ₹799 for the case and first refill, then about ₹249–299 per refill.

**Proof it works abroad:** Wild, UK: Unilever bought it in 2025, in a deal reported to value it at about £230 million ([source](https://startups.co.uk/news/wild-acquired-by-unilever/))

**Who does this in India now:** One found: Bare Necessities, with Without, launched what it calls India's first refillable natural roll-on deodorant ([link](https://barenecessities.in/blogs/about-our-products/launching-indias-first-refillable-natural-roll-on-deodorant)).

**To start:** About ₹2–2.5 lakh (first factory batch, cases, licence and labels, ads). About 8–10 weeks. Main risk: the case must feel premium but stay cheap, and the formula must work in Indian heat.

### 7. Gel nail stickers that set under a small lamp
**In one line:** Soft gel nail stickers you press on at home and harden under a small lamp, like salon gel nails.

**What's missing in India today:** A gel manicure at a salon costs about ₹1,000–2,000 and takes an hour. The nail stickers sold here are thin and peel off within days.

**How it works:**
1. You order designs from a factory that makes "semi-cured" gel strips (mostly in Korea and China), picking colours for weddings, festivals and office wear.
2. You sell a starter kit (strips plus a small lamp) and refill packs online.
3. Customers put them on in about 15 minutes and wear them for up to about two weeks.
4. You launch new designs every month, with Reels showing the before and after.

**Who pays and how much:** Women aged 18–40 who like gel nails but not the salon time or price; about ₹1,499 for a starter kit with lamp, then about ₹599–799 a pack.

**Proof it works abroad:** Refinery29 UK reports that these strips "are replacing salon gels", names UK sellers MoYou London, Maniko and Hello Nails, and says they last around 14 days ([source](https://www.refinery29.com/en-gb/semi-cured-uv-gel-nail-strips))

**Who does this in India now:** No Indian brand found in a quick search.

**To start:** About ₹1.5–2 lakh (first designs, lamps, packaging, ads). About 10–12 weeks. Main risk: imported nail products may need a cosmetics import registration that can take 3 months or more, so check this first or find a maker in India.

### 8. Nordic pole walking groups for people over 45
**In one line:** Guided group walks using special walking poles, which turn a normal walk into a full-body workout.

**What's missing in India today:** Millions of people over 45 walk in parks every morning, but plain walking gets boring and works mostly the legs. Nordic walking, popular in Germany and the UK, is almost unknown here.

2. You buy a set of poles and get 2–3 fitness trainers qualified as Nordic walking instructors.

4. You sell plans and poles, and run a monthly "walk and breakfast" meet-up.

**Who pays and how much:** Men and women aged about 45–70; about ₹1,500 for the 4-week course, then about ₹1,500–2,000 a month.

**Proof it works abroad:** Nordic Walking UK (now WALX), UK: brought Nordic walking to the UK in 2004, has trained thousands of instructors, and says it has helped over half a million people become more active ([source](https://walx.co.uk/about-us/))

**Who does this in India now:** No one found in a quick search.

**To start:** About ₹80,000 (about 30 pairs of poles, instructor training, ads). About 4–5 weeks. Main risk: people may find the poles odd at first, so Reels must show how good the workout feels.

### 9. Sweat-proof undershirts for office men
**In one line:** Cotton undershirts with a hidden sweat-blocking layer under the arms, so sweat never reaches the shirt.

**What's missing in India today:** In humid cities, men get sweat patches and yellow stains on office shirts by late morning. Indian undershirts are plain vests that simply soak through.

**How it works:**
1. You find a garment factory in Tiruppur or Bengaluru to make undershirts with a sewn-in sweat-blocking underarm panel, to your design.
2. You test samples on hot commutes and post "sweat test" Reels.
3. Customers buy packs of 3 online; later you add collar liners that stop collar stains.
4. You sell bulk packs to grooms' families and to companies whose staff wear formal uniforms.

**Who pays and how much:** Office men, sales staff, grooms; about ₹2,499–2,999 for a pack of 3.

**Proof it works abroad:** Thompson Tee, USA: started with $20,000 of the founders' money and had sold 2 million sweat-proof undershirts by 2022 ([source](https://www.thompsontee.com/pages/about-us))

**Who does this in India now:** No Indian brand found in a quick search. US brands such as Ejis are only available through import sites like Ubuy ([Ubuy](https://www.ubuy.co.in/product/2DG7F3P8-ejis-mens-sweat-proof-undershirt-v-neck-anti-odor-silver-micro-modal-sweat-pads)).

**To start:** About ₹1.5–2 lakh (samples, fabric tests, first 600 pieces, ads). About 8–10 weeks. Main risk: getting the sweat panel right, because a bad batch means many returns.

### 10. Fan-cooled jackets for outdoor workers
**In one line:** Light jackets with two small battery fans that blow air under the cloth to keep workers cool.

**What's missing in India today:** Construction workers, delivery riders, traffic police and factory staff work in 40°C-plus heat. Their employers give them water and caps, but little else.

**How it works:**
1. You import fan jackets and vests from a factory (mostly in China or Japan), with the buyer's logo if they want.
2. You sell to companies: builders, delivery firms, factories, event companies and city councils.
3. You run a free one-week trial on a work site so workers and managers feel the difference.
4. You sell spare batteries and fans later.

**Who pays and how much:** Companies buying for their workers; about ₹5,000–8,000 per jacket with battery.

**Proof it works abroad:** Kuchofuku (the name means "air-conditioned clothes"), Japan: has made fan-fitted work clothes for about 35 years and sells suits, vests and half-sleeve versions through Amazon and Rakuten ([source](https://www.9229.co.jp))

**Who does this in India now:** No consumer brand found. Generic fan jackets (about ₹2,300) and industrial cooling vests (Inuteq, Surakshit) are sold on IndiaMART ([IndiaMART](https://m.indiamart.com/impcat/cooling-vests.html)).

**To start:** About ₹2 lakh (samples, about 30 jackets for trials, battery safety checks). About 8–12 weeks to the first company order. Main risk: lithium batteries may need Indian safety approval (BIS) before import, and sales are seasonal, so sell from January for the summer.

### 15. Forest bathing walks
**In one line:** Slow, quiet, guided walks in city forests where you notice the trees, sounds and smells.

**What's missing in India today:** City people are stressed and on their phones all day. Nature walks here are about fitness or birdwatching; slow, guided "forest bathing", common in Japan, is rare.

**How it works:**

4. You sell private walks to companies as a calm team outing.

**Who pays and how much:** Office workers aged 25–50, and companies; about ₹1,200–1,500 per person, or about ₹25,000 for a private company walk.

**Proof it works abroad:** Association of Nature and Forest Therapy (ANFT), USA: has trained forest-bathing guides for over a decade and runs trainings in Japan, Hong Kong, Thailand and Costa Rica ([source](https://www.anft.earth))

**Who does this in India now:** 2–3: luxury resorts (Oberoi Sukhvilas, Pugdundee Safaris) offer it at their properties, and Bengaluru has seen one-off walks in Cubbon Park ([SheThePeople](https://www.shethepeople.tv/news/bangalore-forest-bathing-price-cubbon-park-4491970)). No regular city walks found.

**To start:** About ₹50,000 (guide training or fees, park permissions, ads). About 3–4 weeks. Main risk: it is new and slow, so Reels must show why it feels good; never call it "therapy".

### 18. Grooming visits at home for bedridden and elderly people
**In one line:** A trained team visits homes to give bedridden elderly people a haircut, shave, nail trim and hair-wash.

**What's missing in India today:** Elderly people who cannot leave home often go months without a proper haircut or nail trim. Salon staff are not trained to handle frail people, and home nurses focus on medical care.

**How it works:**
1. The family (often a son or daughter abroad) books one visit or a monthly plan.
2. You train salon professionals in gentle care for elderly clients and give them a portable kit with an inflatable hair-wash basin.

### 96. Rooftop tents for your own SUV, rented by the weekend
**In one line:** A tent fitted on top of your own car for a weekend, so you can camp almost anywhere.

**What's missing in India today:** More people own SUVs like the Thar and Jimny and love road trips, but a rooftop tent is expensive to buy. You can rent a whole campervan or a ground tent, but not a rooftop tent for your own car.

**How it works:**
1. A customer picks dates and tells you the car model.
2. You buy 2–3 rooftop tents, or tie up with a rooftop-tent seller who lends stock and splits the money.
3. A partner fitter mounts the tent on Friday and removes it on Monday.
4. You also rent camping kits (mattress, lights, stove) and share safe camping spots.

**Who pays and how much:** SUV owners aged about 25–45. About ₹3,000–₹5,000 a night, minimum 2 nights.

**Proof it works abroad:** Rooftop-tent hire shops in Australia and the UK (not checked).

**Who does this in India now:** Camping Co (Guwahati) rents Thars and Xenons that come with rooftop tents ([Responsible Tourism India](https://www.responsibletourismindia.com/stay/camping-co/309)); campervan firms such as Motorhome Adventures rent whole vans; Renta.in rents ground tents. No one found renting a rooftop tent for your own car.

**To start:** About ₹1,50,000–₹2,00,000 if you buy 2 tents, or about ₹40,000 if a seller lends stock. About 4 weeks to the first paying customer. Main risk: tents don't fit every car's roof; damage.

### 97. Checked second-hand premium cycles and e-cycles with a warranty
**In one line:** Buy a used premium bicycle or e-cycle that has been checked, fixed and comes with a 6-month warranty.

**What's missing in India today:** Many people buy costly cycles and e-cycles, ride them for a few months, then leave them unused. Buyers on OLX fear hidden faults and stolen cycles, so good used cycles don't sell easily.

**How it works:**
1. Owners send photos; you offer a price and buy the cycle, or list it for them and take a share when it sells.
2. A partner cycle mechanic checks and fixes it (brakes, gears, and the battery on e-cycles), and you photograph it.
3. You sell it online and on Instagram with a 6-month warranty and home delivery.
4. Later you offer trade-ins: bring your old cycle and pay the difference for a better one.

**Who pays and how much:** Office commuters, fitness riders and students. About ₹8,000–₹40,000 a cycle; you keep about ₹2,000–₹6,000 on each.

**Proof it works abroad:** Upway, France and USA: has refurbished and sold over 100,000 e-bikes, usually about 45% below the new price, with a one-year warranty ([Tech.eu](https://tech.eu/2025/11/07/upway-raises-60m-to-refurbish-1-million-e-bikes-by-2030/))

**Who does this in India now:** No one found for bicycles in a quick search. CredR sells checked second-hand motorbikes and scooters, not cycles.

**To start:** About ₹1,00,000–₹1,50,000 for the first 10–15 cycles, or close to nothing if you start by listing cycles for owners. About 3–4 weeks to the first sale. Main risk: being sold stolen cycles; slow sales.

### 98. A bicycle repair van that comes to your home or office
**In one line:** A mechanic comes to your society parking or office and services your bicycle or e-cycle on the spot.

**What's missing in India today:** Cycle shops are often far away and you need a car to carry the cycle there, so cycles sit with flat tyres and rusty chains. E-cycle owners find it especially hard to get someone who understands their cycle.

3. You sell yearly service plans and spare parts.
4. You set up "cycle service days" with housing societies and offices.

**Who pays and how much:** Cycle owners, parents of young riders, office cyclists and e-cycle owners. About ₹499–₹999 a service.

**Who does this in India now:** 1: Fix My Cycle (Chennai) books doorstep bicycle repairs in about 20 cities ([YourStory](https://yourstory.com/2020/01/chennai-startup-fix-my-cycle-doorstep-service)).

### 99. Washable cups for events instead of plastic
**In one line:** You bring washable cups to events, collect them afterwards and get them washed, so no plastic cups are used.

**What's missing in India today:** Concerts, marathons, festivals and society events throw away thousands of plastic and paper cups. Organisers want to look green but don't have time to manage reusable cups themselves.

**How it works:**
1. An event organiser books cups for a date; you bring branded hard cups in crates.
2. Guests pay a small deposit with their first drink and get it back when they return the cup, or keep the cup.
3. A commercial dishwashing partner collects the dirty crates and washes them for the next event.
4. You sell the event's sponsor a logo printed on the cups.

**Who pays and how much:** Event organisers, breweries, marathon organisers and companies holding events. About ₹8–₹15 a cup per use, plus guest deposits.

**Proof it works abroad:** RECUP, Germany: its deposit cups are used at around 20,000 places across Germany, with a €1 deposit a cup ([RECUP](https://recup.de/en/))

**Who does this in India now:** Recube, Mumbai (brand Cupable): supplies reusable cups to 500+ food outlets and has been used at 150+ events ([Eco-Business](https://www.eco-business.com/ms/news/reuse-and-refill-a-business-model-turning-the-tide-on-single-use-plastic/)).

**To start:** About ₹1,00,000 (about 3,000 cups, crates, ads). About 4 weeks to the first paying customer (December events). Main risk: cups going missing; organisers choosing cheap plastic instead.

### 100. Rent a whole fruit tree and get all its fruit
**In one line:** Book one apple, orange or litchi tree on a farm for a season and get all its fruit delivered.

**What's missing in India today:** People want fresh, naturally ripened fruit and to know who grew it. One startup does this for mangoes, but other fruits have no such option.

**How it works:**
1. You sign up a few orchards (for example, apples in Himachal or oranges near Nagpur), and the farmer tags and photographs each tree.
2. Customers choose a tree online, pay for the season, and get photo and video updates from the farm.
3. At harvest the farmer picks and packs the fruit, and a courier partner delivers it in 2–3 boxes.
4. You sell "a tree in your name" as a birthday or Diwali gift.

**Who pays and how much:** City families and gift buyers. About ₹6,000–₹12,000 a tree for a season (about 20–40 kg of fruit).

**Proof it works abroad:** Nudo Adopt, Italy: people adopt an olive tree from small farmers and receive its olive oil by post every few months; it marked its 20th anniversary in 2025 ([Nudo Adopt](https://www.nudoadopt.com/))

**Who does this in India now:** Rent a Tree (Kochi) rents Alphonso mango trees from about ₹10,300 a season and had about 160 customers in March 2026 ([FreshPlaza](https://www.freshplaza.com/north-america/article/9816016/why-buy-mangoes-when-you-can-rent-the-whole-tree-in-india/)). It only does mangoes.

**To start:** About ₹50,000 (farm visits, tagging, photos, website, ads). About 4–6 weeks to the first paying customer (sell before each harvest). Main risk: bad weather cuts the crop; fruit damaged in transit.
